## THE HOOK

227 days. That's how long your family will wait for probate while property taxes, insurance, and maintenance costs drain the estate—every single month.

The BC Supreme Court probate backlog isn't just a bureaucratic inconvenience. It's a financial crisis for executors managing real estate. While you wait for the Grant of Probate, the clock is ticking: $4,200 in annual property taxes, $1,800 in insurance, $600 in utilities, and the constant worry that an empty house is vulnerable to break-ins, frozen pipes, or neighborhood complaints.

You didn't ask for this responsibility. But now you're standing between your family's expectations and a system that's moving at a glacial pace. Here's what you need to know to protect the estate—and your sanity.

## THE THREE-POINT VALUE PROPOSITION

Here are the three critical realities every BC executor must understand about the 227-day probate timeline:

### 1. The Cash Flow Crisis is Real (And It Starts Immediately)

The moment someone passes away, the financial obligations don't stop—but your access to their accounts does.

**The Math**: A typical BC estate property carries $6,600+ in annual holding costs. That's $550 per month while you wait for probate. Over 227 days (7.5 months), you're looking at $4,125 in out-of-pocket expenses before you can even list the property.

**The Problem**: Most executors don't have $4,000+ sitting around to float an estate. You might be tempted to use estate funds from the deceased's account, but without the Grant of Probate, banks won't release a single dollar. This creates an impossible situation: you're legally responsible for maintaining the property, but you have no legal access to the money to do it.

**The Solution**: Bridge financing exists specifically for this situation. Estate bridge loans allow you to cover holding costs using the property as collateral, with repayment coming from the estate once probate is granted. Interest rates are higher than traditional mortgages (typically 8-12%), but it's often the only way to avoid paying out of pocket or letting the property deteriorate.

### 2. The 227-Day Timeline Has Hidden Pressure Points

Probate isn't a smooth 227-day wait. It's a series of deadlines, waiting periods, and potential complications that can extend the timeline even further.

**The Timeline Breakdown**:

- **Days 1-30**: Locate the Will, secure the property, notify beneficiaries, and gather estate documents
- **Days 31-60**: File Form P2 with BC Supreme Court (includes original Will, death certificate, estate inventory)
- **Days 61-150**: Court review period (this is where the 227-day average comes from—backlogs create unpredictable delays)
- **Days 151-180**: Creditor claim period (legally required 210-day window from Grant issuance)
- **Days 181-227**: Final distribution preparation

**The Pressure Points**:

- If the Will is contested, add 6-12 months
- If beneficiaries can't be located, add 3-6 months
- If the estate inventory is incomplete, the court will reject your application and you start over
- If there's a mortgage or lien dispute, resolution must happen before probate can proceed

**The Strategy**: Work backwards from the 227-day average. Assume delays and build buffer time into your family's expectations. The worst thing you can do is promise beneficiaries a quick distribution—under-promise and over-deliver.

### 3. The Empty House is Your Biggest Liability

An empty estate property isn't just a financial drain—it's a legal and emotional minefield.

**The Risks**:

- **Insurance Issues**: Most home insurance policies have a 30-60 day vacancy clause. After that, your coverage drops dramatically or disappears entirely. If something happens to the property (fire, flood, vandalism), you could be personally liable.
- **Municipal Violations**: Overgrown lawns, snow removal, and property maintenance are your responsibility. Municipalities can fine the estate—and those fines come out of the beneficiaries' inheritance.
- **Security Concerns**: Empty homes are targets. Break-ins, squatters, and vandalism are common. Neighbors notice when a house sits dark for months.
- **Deterioration**: Homes need to be lived in. Frozen pipes, mold, pest infestations, and mechanical failures accelerate when a property sits vacant.

**The Protection Plan**:

1. **Notify insurance immediately** and get vacant home coverage (expect to pay 2-3x normal premiums)
2. **Install a monitored security system** with cameras and motion sensors
3. **Arrange weekly property checks** (hire a property management company or ask a trusted neighbor)
4. **Keep utilities on** (heat, water, electricity)—the cost is far less than the damage from frozen pipes
5. **Consider a house sitter** or short-term rental (with proper legal advice) to keep the property occupied

## THE EMOTIONAL STORY

Last month, I sat across from David at a coffee shop in Surrey. His mother had passed away three months earlier, leaving him as executor of her estate. The house—a beautiful 1970s rancher in Cloverdale worth $850,000—sat empty while he waited for probate.

"I thought I was doing everything right," he said, pulling out a folder of receipts. "I filed the paperwork on time. I hired a lawyer. But nobody told me it would take this long."

The receipts told the story: $1,200 in property taxes (first installment), $450 in insurance (vacant home premium), $180 in utilities, $300 for lawn care, $600 for a plumber (frozen pipe burst in January). Total: $2,730 out of his own pocket in just three months.

"My sister keeps asking when we can sell," David continued. "She needs the money for her kids' tuition. My brother thinks I'm stalling. And I'm just... stuck. I can't access Mom's accounts. I can't sell the house. I can't even get the mail forwarded without the Grant."

We walked through his options. The bridge loan would cost him about $850 in interest over the remaining four months, but it would stop the bleeding from his personal account. The property management company would handle the weekly checks for $200/month—expensive, but cheaper than the risk of another frozen pipe.

Six months later, David called me. The Grant had finally come through—day 241, two weeks longer than the average. The house sold in 11 days for $863,000, $13,000 over asking. After all the holding costs, legal fees, and bridge loan interest, the estate netted $798,000 for the three beneficiaries.

"I wish someone had told me about the 227 days on day one," David said. "I would have planned differently. I would have set expectations differently. I wouldn't have felt like I was failing my family every time my sister called."

The probate timeline isn't your fault. But how you navigate it—that's where you can make a difference.
